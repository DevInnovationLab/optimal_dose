#2 / #5 / #9 — Vaccine throughput and compartment normalisation

Feedback from Refine
“To stretch such a modest capacity, the optimal uniform dose ends up being about half of a full dose. Distributing these smaller doses more quickly allows the planner to avert 44% of deaths.”

Refine: the displayed SEIRDV system does not implement this acceleration because vaccination flow is q v_a(t), independent of dose strength; if q is full-dose-equivalent antigen capacity, recipient flow must depend on x_a.
Refine: the vaccination equations include x_a only through efficacy. The paper later measures supply in full-dose equivalents, so fractional dosing should increase recipients per unit time (for a uniform dose, ordinarily proportional to q/x). If the simulations already do this, the mapping is missing from the paper.
Refine: the paper does not state a normalization under which both the transmission and vaccination terms have consistent units. If I_a is within-cohort prevalence, the vaccination term needs a cohort-population conversion; if compartments are masses or shares of the total population, the force of infection needs cohort-size normalization unless it is already embedded in c_{a,a′}.
What I did
There was a need for a more direct explanation/notation of how vaccinations expand as dose shrinks. (Previously we had adjustment of supply explained in words and equations just assumed some flow of daily vaccinations. Now there is a bit of notation, but I tried not to complicate it much.) Also a few words of clarification on how we normalise the sizes of compartments: when working with such a model you can either normalise each age group to 1 and remember their relative sizes, or just normalise the whole population to 1.

#7 — Vaccination of recovered people

Feedback from Refine
Refine: because vaccinated recovered people remain in R_a and no vaccination status is recorded for them, the leakage term can sample the same recovered mass repeatedly while that cohort remains active. This may waste capacity more than once per person and can change rollout timing across dosing policies.
What I did
I added a bit of a comment on this. The assumption that we do not know who was infected and therefore we vaccinated the recovered, that's by design. The assumption that sometimes (very infrequently) we have someone who is vaccinated, infected, then recovered, then they may be vaccinated again---that's just to keep code simple. We could account for it and it my recollection is that this makes no difference to what we're modeling. However, there was a mistake in my code related to this assumption (some minor outflows were not matched by inflows)! So I had to re-run everything.

#29 / #31 / #40 — Local calibration prose

Feedback from Refine
Refine: individual efficacy of 95% does not cap aggregate deaths averted at 95%, because successful vaccination also reduces transmission and indirectly protects others.
Refine: the footnote attributes time variation from distancing and lockdowns to R₀. Under the usual distinction, those interventions change a control/effective reproduction number; variants or baseline contact environments can change the context-specific basic reproduction number.
Refine: the introduction says seven age cohorts, but Section 6.2 specifies eight total cohorts: one child cohort plus seven vaccine-eligible adult cohorts. The seven-dimensional dose vector counts only adults.

#45 / #46 — Literature claims

Feedback from Refine
Refine: the paper is described as using a single age threshold, but Matrajt et al. instead optimise allocation of one and two doses across five age groups; optimal allocations can involve multiple groups rather than one threshold.
Refine: Benest et al. is cited as evidence that sigmoid dose-response curves fit well, but the paper reportedly found adenoviral-vector vaccine data substantially more consistent with a peaking than a saturating response.
What I did
I used LLMs to review these two papers for me as I am pressed for time. But I also remembered these two papers a bit from working on this back in the day. I think Matrajt et al 2021 can be removed from the citation where it was present, because it's doing something different to us. It's about choosing between 1 and 2 doses, very important work and related to our interest, but just not where it previously appeared in the paper. We do cite it elsewhere in the paper Benest is a more important case. Important to also remember that there is immune response (e.g. "is there a peak in measured antibodies?") and then there is efficacy ("is there a peak in protection from disease?").
