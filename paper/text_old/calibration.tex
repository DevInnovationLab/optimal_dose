In this section, we present optimal dosing in a model with heterogeneous harm under a subjective choice of dose-response function, at different levels of dose supply constraints. Our calibration is based on the initial efficacy results of the first effective COVID-19 vaccines and mass vaccination campaigns in late 2020 and throughout 2021, when all countries faced shortage of vaccines. We are interested in comparing three possible approaches to vaccination: giving full doses only, giving an optimal partial dose (not differentiated by risk), and varying the optimal dose as a function of harm.

Previously, in \cite{wiecek2021}, we demonstrated that fractional dosing with a fixed dose (that is, same lower dose used for all) can have large benefits under a range of realistic scenarios stylised after early COVID epidemics. Here, we expand on that work in two major ways. First, we want to characterise the solutions for harm-specific optimal dose: when risk is heterogeneous, how heterogeneous is the dose? Second, we investigate magnitude of marginal benefits of moving beyond fixed optimal dose to an age-specific one. We also extend the implications beyond single epidemic peak to long-run dynamics, where over time individuals lose immunity and are re-vaccinated.


\section{Calibration model}

To solve for optimal dose under variety of scenarios, we use a slightly modified version of the model we previously presented in detail in \cite{wiecek_2021}. Here we just give a short exposition. 

We account for the dynamics of infection, mortality, and vaccination through a SEIRD-type compartmental epidemiological model, which is the workhorse of mathematical epidemiology.\footnote{We solve the model numerically using the \textit{odin} package in R \cite{odin}. While some simple compartmental models admit closed-form solutions, most realistic models, including ours, are too complex to solve analytically due to multiple compartments, age structure, and time-varying parameters. Therefore, we rely on numerical simulation to study the model's behavior and evaluate objective function for the dose optimisation problem.} 

The model divides the population into several compartments: susceptible ($S$), exposed ($E$), infectious ($I$), recovered ($R$), dead ($D$), vaccinated and protected ($P$), and vaccinated but not protected ($N$). Individuals move between these compartments based on a set of differential equations. The population is further stratified into age cohorts, as we will detail below.

The basic epidemic model is as follows. The rate at which susceptible individuals ($S + N$) become infected depends on their contact rates with infectious individuals, which are governed by a fixed contact matrix that specifies the frequency of interactions between different age groups. Once infected, individuals move from the exposed to the infectious compartment at a rate determined by the incubation period of the virus. They then either recover or die, with the proportion of deaths determined by age-specific infection fatality rates (IFRs). 

The basic reproductive number, $\mathcal{R}_0$, represents the average number of new infections generated by a single infected individual in a fully susceptible population. We consider two scenarios: a fast-growing outbreak ($\mathcal{R}_0 = 2$; in absence of vaccine infections peaking at about four months, about 55\% of population infected) and a slower-growing case ($\mathcal{R}_0 = 1.1$, infections slowly rise over 6 months, about 15\% of population infected). For an epidemic with $\mathcal{R}_0 = 1.1$ it's relatively easy to stop exponential growth by making about one in ten individuals immune.

Outputs for a few compartments of the model with $\mathcal{R}_0 = 2$, at different vaccination speeds (more on which below) are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:example_model}. The model parameters(contact rates, incubation and infectious periods, pre-existing immunity, IFRs etc.) are based on empirical evidence for COVID-19, but, given the large heterogeneity in all parameters across countries, the example should be thought of as mainly illustrative. 

The initial cases we present assume no loss of immunity with time, which is appropriate for modeling a single epidemic outbreak. We revisit this assumption later in this section.



\subsection{Vaccine supply and vaccination model}

We consider vaccination of seven age groups: 20-29, 30-39, 40-49, 50-59, 60-69, 70-79, and 80+. We assume that under-20s do not get vaccinated since vaccines were initially approved for adults only. We assume vaccine hesitancy of 20\% in each age group, i.e. 80\% of people are willing to get vaccinated.\footnote{Overall, 80\% was the upper limit of vaccination coverage during COVID, but note that we use the same coverage in all age groups. In 2021 it was typical to reach over 80\% in highest risk groups and much lower in general population, but this pattern differed considerably across countries.}

We contrast two vaccination problems: ``dynamic'', where vaccinations occur during the epidemic, and ``static'', where vaccinations occur before the epidemic. The dynamic case is intended as a more realistic one in the case of COVID, but we also include the static case for contrast and because it's more flexible. In both cases we use the same SEIRD model.

In dynamic cases, the number of vaccinations is constant every day (e.g. 1\% of adult population vaccinated every day), to reflect supply and logistical constraints. Vaccination is reflected by moving individuals from the susceptible and recovered compartments to the $P$ compartment: each day we vaccinate a set proportion of total population $\delta$; a fraction $\phi(x)$ (efficacy) moves to protected compartment $P$ and remaining $1-\phi(x)$ move to compartment $N$.

While the details varied from country to country, to our knowledge all country-wide vaccination campaigns prioritised higher risk individuals. Therefore, to keep this example realistic, for dynamic cases we use a strict prioritisation strategy: 10-year age bands are vaccinated in descending order, each of them up to a point when there are no more willing individuals. 

In static cases, we consider several strategies for selecting age-group-specific dosage. This scheme is more flexible than in the dynamic cases: since we do not have to consider the order in which groups are vaccinated, prioritisation is meaningless. What matters is how the finite supply is divided among individuals.

This is done by changing the starting condition of the ODE system so that appropriate fraction of individuals are placed in $P$ and $N$ compartments. We discuss the potential loss of (natural and vaccine-induced) immunity later, but ignore it in the initial examples.

To keep these cases illustrative, we vary the quantity of vaccine available every day (dynamic cases) or total supply (static cases) through a wide range of values. This covers the heterogeneity in country-level supplies which occured during the COVID pandemic. For example, Israel was able to vaccinate a large fraction of its population in a manner of weeks, while most countries were only able to secure a very small supply of vaccine, which was then reserved for priority groups. In many cases these supply constraints were not alleviated until 2022. However, we also include the more extreme examples for illustrative purposes. To give some more precise examples for COVID:

\begin{itemize}

\item Wealthy countries that were at the front of the queue initially (circa January 2021) had enough doses to vaccinate a few percent of their population
\item As vaccination campaigns took off, these countries were vaccinating between 0.5\% and 1\% of their population. The UK and US fully vaccinated half of the population in the first half of 2021. Many EU countries started slowly and took several more months to reach that milestone. Typically the coverage of 60\%-80\% (depending on hesitancy) was reached within one year.
\item In poorer countries that had to rely on COVAX and donations, there was initially no supply; once vaccines were available, the vaccination rates were very slow. India (with large domestic supply) took 13 months to reach 50\% fully vaccinated, but Nigeria vaccinated 2\% in 2021 and then 25\% in 2022. 

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dose-response function}

In these simulations we make two important simplifying assumptions. First, the vaccine works equally well to prevent infection (also known as providing sterilising immunity), disease, and severe disease. We empirically know that this was not the case for COVID vaccines, especially with emergence of variants: vaccines stayed effective at preventing deaths and hospitalisations, but less so at preventing transmission.\footnote{There are three good reasons to make that assumption in this section: (1) it simplifies presentation and allows us to generalise from this example, (2) efficacy against infection was heavily dependent on viral variants (which we do not model), so there isn't a canonical value we could use (3) even with the assumption of sterilising immunity in place, the optimal solutions tend toward dosing that is proportional to mortality risk anyway.} Second, we assume a ``non-leaky'' vaccine: there is no partial protection and following a vaccination individuals are either protected (move to compartment $P$) or not (move to $N$). 

For the dose-response function, we use $\phi(x) = Ax^\beta + Bx$ for $x \in [0,\infty)$. Our choice of $\phi(x)$ is twice differentiable and concave. We chose a subjective dose-response function as follows: first, assume that vaccine developers set full doses, $x^m=1$, to maximize expected net benefits such that $\phi'(1)=0$. At a full dose, we assumed the vaccine course would yield 95\% of maximum net benefits, $\phi(1)=0.95$, which is roughly level of efficacy that has been estimated for Moderna and Pfizer vaccines in December 2020; both vaccines have also reported no serious adverse events.

Second, at a half dose, $x=0.5$, we assumed some loss of efficacy, down to 80\% of maximum net benefits, $\phi(0.5)=0.8$. We then assumed that minimal acceptable level of efficacy (according to WHO's target product profile for COVID-19 vaccines) occurs at one-fifth of the dose and set $\phi(0.2) = 0.5$. At $x=0$, we expected the vaccine to yield no net benefits, $\phi(0)=0$.  Together these imply parameters $A=-25.3$, $\beta = 1.04$, and $B=26.3$. 

Our subjective choice of dose-response for this example is motivated by trial data and analysis we conducted in \cite{wiecek_pnas_2021}. Our analysis in that paper suggested that the subjective choice of dose-response curve for this example can be thought of as conservative with regards to loss of efficacy, but it's important to remember that this ignores viral variants.



\subsection{Heterogeneity in harm of infection and death}

We assume a discrete distribution of harm, which is driven by a combination of two risks; (1) a risk of infection: dynamic with time and age-specific (governed by the epidemiological model); (2) a risk death if infected (IFR): static, but also age-specific. Both IFR and infection risks vary in 10-year age bands. Older people have a much higher IFR than the younger adults (about 10\% in 80+ compared to 0.1\% in 30-40), see Figure~\ref{fig:age_solutions_lic_hic} for illustration.\footnote{While IFR varied from country to country and also changed over time, these simple estimates capture the main feature we are interested in illustrating here, the steep increase with age. The patterns also look different in low-income countries compared to high-income countries, more on which below.} 

A demonstration of an evolution of a single dynamic model is given in Figure 1. This is done under a fast-growing epidemic ($R_0 = 2$). We can clearly see that in this case a vaccination can have a decisive impact if enough people acquire vaccine-induced protection early enough in the outbreak.


\section{Magnitude of benefits from optimising}

For the rest of this section, we use non-linear optimisation solver to find optimal doses under a variety of scenarios.\footnote{We solve the differntial equations numerically using the \textit{odin} package in R. While some simple compartmental models admit closed-form solutions, most realistic models, including ours, are too complex to solve analytically, due to multiple compartments, age structure, and time-varying parameters. Therefore, we rely on numerical simulation to study the model's behavior and make quantitative predictions.} In the base case we vary:

\begin{itemize}
\item daily vaccination speed (if vaccinating during epidemic) or total vaccine quantity (if vaccinating before),
\item slow-growing epidemic ($\mathcal{R}_0 = 1.1$) and fast-growing epidemic ($\mathcal{R}_0 = 2$),
\end{itemize}

We can summarise the base case results in two ways: the magnitude of reductions and distribution with age. Figure~\ref{fig:obj_f} focuses on magnitude, i.e. how the objective function (mortality) changes as if we optimise doses. This is crucial, because it directly addresses the central question of whether dose-stretching has potential save lives. We contrast four increasingly complex strategies of dosing:

\begin{itemize}

\item using full doses (e.g., if supply is 10\% that means the top 10\% of the population with highest risk are vaccinated);
\item age-invariant fractional doses: we find the optimal fractional dose and vaccinate everyone with it until the supply is exhausted;\footnote{For static cases, we allow for some age groups to be excluded from vaccination altogether, meaning that the optimisation is done by generating all permutations of which age groups are included, sharing vaccine equally among all included groups, and then calculating the value of the objective function. For dynamic case the prioritisation is already built in, therefore we simply optimise the single parameter, $x$.}
\item age-specific optimal dose: we find a 7-dimensional vector of optimal doses, $(x_{[20,30)}, \allowbreak x_{[30, 40)}, \ldots, x_{\geq 80})$, with each vector component varying freely.\footnote{To solve the non-linear optimisation problem, we use R package \textit{nloptr} with tolerance set to 1e-06. We find that this provides precise results with short runtimes.}

\end{itemize}

When lowering doses in dynamic cases, we assume that daily number of vaccinees rises in inverse proportion to dose: e.g. if using 0.5 dose for all, we can vaccinate twice as many people every day. In other words, we do not consider any logistical constraints.

Figure~\ref{fig:obj_f} contrasts fast-growth and slow-growth epidemics (left and right panels). It shows proportion of deaths reduced relative to no vaccination (top and middle panels) and further improvements relative to the full dose strategy (bottom). Predictably, we find that vaccination with an effective vaccine can avert a large proportion of deaths (Figure~\ref{fig:obj_f}, upper panels, black line); because the full doses are highly effective, at a certain level of coverage herd immunity is reached and there are nearly no deaths. Although we can't see that on the graph (which uses relative quantities), for a slowly growing epidemic with $\mathcal{R}_0 = 1.1$ that point is achieved quickly since there is no exponential growth once about 10\% of population is immune. 

Secondly, in presence of supply constraints there are very large benefits from fractionation. Relative to full dose, the fractional dosing strategies are U-shaped functions of supply (Figure~\ref{fig:obj_f}, bottom panel). This matches observations from our earlier paper, but now with some further results on age-specific optimal dosing. While there are always some gains from making dosing age-specific, compared to the ``uniform'' (age-invariant) fractional dose (due to optimising over more parameters), the magnitude of further improvements is very small. As we can see in Table~\ref{table:obj_f}), in the most relevant cases of heavily constrained supply, the marginal gain from making dosing age-specific is 1-2\% of deaths averted.\footnote{For the dynamic scenario (not shown in the table for conciseness) the marginal gains are of the same magnitude.}

To see how the improvements are achieved and why they are only meaningful when supply is severly limited, we now look at the age-optimal solutions themselves. These are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:age_solutions_both}.

When vaccinating before the epidemic (upper panels), the optimal strategy tends to distribute limited supply of vaccine to groups with high mortality risk and ignore the ``transmitting groups'', i.e. most solution vectors rise with age. That is because of steep differences in mortality risk and the fact that small quantities of vaccine are not able to slow the spread, even assuming sterilising immunity.\footnote{For slowly-growing epidemics with very small supply, we find an exception to this, where working-age adults are targetted for vaccination ahead of the elderly. That is because for it's easier to blunt the exponential growth when $\mathcal{R}_0$ is smaller. As we saw in Figure~\ref{fig:obj_f} a large majority of deaths can be averted by using lower doses.} When supply is very low, the optimal solution is proportional to IFR, which we plot on the top left panel in black dotted line.

For the dynamic problem (Figure~\ref{fig:age_solutions_both}, lower panels), the optimal dose also rises with age when supply is constrained. (We do not show optimal dose in groups with less than 10\% coverage before the peak of the epidemic.) For particularly fast vaccination campaigns (3-6 months) it assumes an U shape; an intuitive explanation is that this is done to extend protection to youngest adults in order to slow down transmission. 

In general it seems the large differences in dosing in heterogeneous population (in both static and dynamic cases) occur in the case of severe supply constraints. When supply is really short, the optimal strategy is roughly proportional to mortality risk. When there is more supply, the optimal solutions tend to slope upwards with age, although often less steeply. However, in cases where the vaccine has impact on transmission (which we assumed here), there are no hard rules, because the protection against disease in those at highest risk of mortality has to be traded off against benefits of breaking transmission, which occurs in working age adults. Thus in realistic cases the vector of optimal dosing is not always monotonic with risk

\section{Additonal considerations}


\subsection{More homogeneous risk structure}

Our base case model used IFR estimates from high-income countries, steeply rising with age. However, in low-income countries, while the overall age-averaged COVID mortality was often higher than in HICs (due to health care factors), various estimates suggested that the IFR as a function of age was flatter than in the HICs (Figure~\ref{fig:age_solutions_lic_hic}, right panel), e.g. due to fewer comorbidities.

Therefore we repeated our analysis with flatter IFR structure, typical of LICs. Left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:age_solutions_lic_hic} compares the optimal solutions in LICs and HICs; for brevity we show only the static case with $\mathcal{R}_0=2$. 
There are two main differences. First, large portion of supply is allocated to younger adults, presumably due to transmission-breaking properties. Consequentially, with flatter IFR vector the optimal doses are also comparably less variable with age than in the base case, at all levels of supply.

In the base case we analysed a single epidemic outbreak, assuming no loss of immunity over time, either from vaccination or natural infection. However, for many infectious diseases immunity will wane quickly enough to lead to re-infections. For COVID and influenza this risk can be managed by booster vaccinations. 

Fully capturing these dynamics in even a complex compartmental model is difficult, for three reasons. First, each vaccination and infection compounds the population heterogeneity by stimulation of immune memory, which renders the assumption of leaky vaccine even more simplistic. This in turn is further complicated by emergence of different strains and viral drift, leading to effectiveness of vaccines changing over time. Second, high-quality data required to inform all of these quantities are not easy to obtain and analysing them requires a lot of judgement calls. Third, and perhaps most importantly, beyond short-term efficacy and side effects, reducing the dose can also lead to shorter duration of protection. 
  
With that in mind, we provide only an illustrative modification of our base case model, adapted to multiple seasons. In the modified model, individuals in the recovered ($R$) and ``vaccinated, protected'' ($P$) compartments gradually lose their immunity and move back to the susceptible ($S$) compartment at a rate determined by the duration of immunity. We assume the average length of protection to be 365 days. The loss of immunity leads to a periodical resurgence of infections, as the pool of susceptible individuals grows, much as we observe for COVID or influenza. To counter this, we introduce recurring vaccinations, where individuals are re-vaccinated at regular intervals to maintain a high level of population immunity. We still use the simplistic dichotomy of non-leaky vaccine. This abstracts from some relevant real world issues, but does provide an interesting illustration of some issues.

This dynamic is illustrated over four years in Figure~\ref{fig:example_multiseason}. The oscillatory behaviour of infection risk is instantly apparent in the left panel. If there is little vaccination happening (red line), the seasonal amplitude decreases over time. If an effective vaccine is available fast (blue line), the initial wave is prevented, but there is a perceptible ``rebound'' in subsequent waves. For an endemic disease, preventing infections during a single outbreak means that the pool of susceptible individuals is going to be larger during the next period of exponential growth. This problem can be eliminated (or exacerbated) by timing the vaccination accordingly, which is not done in this example (Figure~\ref{fig:example_multiseason}, right panel).

Over longer term the total mortality in both scenarios is similar (Figure~\ref{fig:example_multiseason}, middle panel); that is, if we ignore time discounting the benefits of a policy that optimises for reduced short-term infections are net zero. We showed elsewhere using a more realistic model that this ``rebound'' effect has a sizeable impact on estimates of vaccine benefits in COVID epidemics (Więcek et al 2023).
